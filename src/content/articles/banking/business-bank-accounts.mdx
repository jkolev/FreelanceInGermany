## Do you need a separate business account?

Not by law. A freelancer who works alone can use a private account (<Term de="Girokonto" />) for business payments. There are good reasons to open a separate business account (<Term de="Geschäftskonto" />) anyway:

- **Bookkeeping is easier.** Your accounting app or tax advisor only sees business payments, and every transaction in the account belongs in your profit calculation (<Term de="Einnahmenüberschussrechnung" />).
- **Your bank may require it.** Many banks' terms don't allow regular business use of a private account, and they can close an account that is used that way.
- **Setting money aside is simpler.** Several business accounts let you move a share of each payment into a sub-account for tax and VAT, so the money is there when your prepayments and returns are due.

If you have a trade business (<Term de="Gewerbe" />) or plan to hire people, a business account becomes even more useful. See [Freiberufler vs. Gewerbe](/start/freiberufler-vs-gewerbe).

## What to look for

- **English app and support.** All four main options here have an English app. Support quality varies, so test it before you move all your payments.
- **IBAN country.** A German IBAN starts with DE. EU rules forbid refusing an account just because it is in another EU country, but a German IBAN avoids friction with the occasional client, landlord or authority that still insists on one.
- **Deposit protection.** At a bank, deposits are protected up to €100,000 per customer and bank (<Term de="Einlagensicherung" />). E-money and payment institutions don't offer this; they must keep customer money separate from their own, which protects it if the company fails, but not in the same way.
- **Transfers included.** Free plans often include only a few transfers a month. Count how many invoices you are paid for and how many bills you pay.
- **Cash.** Most app banks handle cash poorly. If clients pay you in cash, check the deposit options and fees first.
- **Tools.** Invoicing, receipt capture, tax sub-accounts and a DATEV export for your tax advisor can replace a separate app. Our [accounting software comparison](/taxes/accounting-software) covers the apps that do this best.

## Kontist

Kontist is built for freelancers. Its app estimates the income tax and VAT you will owe on each payment and can set the money aside for you. Accounts are held at Solaris, a German bank, so deposits are protected up to €100,000.

- **Free, €0:** German IBAN, physical Visa debit card, 5 free transfers a month (then €0.25 each). Cash withdrawals cost €2 each.
- **Start, €11 a month:** 30 transfers, sub-accounts, 2 free cash withdrawals a month.
- **Plus, €25 a month:** 100 transfers, 4 free withdrawals and accounting functions.

**Good to know:** card payments in foreign currencies cost 1.5% to 2%, depending on the plan. Kontist also sells a done-for-you tax service with a tax advisor for €179 a month, described in our [accounting software comparison](/taxes/accounting-software).

## N26 Business

N26 is a German bank, so deposits are covered by the German deposit guarantee. Its business accounts are for freelancers and sole traders who work under their own name; the account and card show your name, not a company name.

- **Business Standard, free:** German IBAN, virtual Mastercard, 2 free ATM withdrawals a month in the eurozone, and 0.1% cashback on card payments.
- **Paid plans:** add a physical card at no extra charge, more free ATM withdrawals and other perks. Check the current prices on N26's website.

**Good to know:** on the free plan, the physical card costs a one-off €10. You open a personal N26 account first and add the business account in the app. N26 has no DATEV export, so it suits freelancers who do their own bookkeeping or use an app that connects to the account.

## Qonto

Qonto is a French bank, licensed by the European Central Bank and supervised in France, with a German branch. Deposits are protected under the French deposit guarantee, which also covers up to €100,000.

- **Starter, free (for freelancers and sole traders):** German IBAN, 5 transfers a month, a virtual card, compliant e-invoicing and DATEV/Lexware sync.
- **Basic, €9 a month billed yearly:** 30 transfers a month, a physical card plus 2 virtual cards, payment links and 24/7 support.
- **Smart, €19 a month billed yearly:** adds sub-accounts, interest on your balance and access for your accountant.

**Good to know:** e-invoicing is included even on the free plan, which helps from 2027 and 2028, when sending e-invoices to German business clients becomes mandatory. See [how to write a German invoice](/taxes/invoice).

## Finom

Finom offers a wide set of features on its free plan, including invoicing, e-invoices and accounting integrations. Finom Payments is an e-money institution regulated by De Nederlandsche Bank, the Dutch central bank, not a bank, so balances are not covered by the deposit guarantee. Customer money must be kept separate from Finom's own instead.

- **Solo, free:** German IBAN, a virtual card, invoicing and e-invoices, and free transfers up to a monthly limit.
- **Paid plans:** higher limits, physical cards and cashback. Check the current prices on Finom's pricing page.

**Good to know:** if you keep large balances, the missing deposit guarantee matters more. Some freelancers keep their savings and tax reserves at a bank and use Finom for day-to-day payments.

## Other options

- **Holvi:** a Finnish payment institution with German IBANs for customers in Germany, and plans that combine banking with invoicing and bookkeeping. It is not a bank, so there is no deposit guarantee. Prices differ by country, so check the German price page.
- **FYRST:** a Deutsche Bank brand with a free entry plan, cash deposits at Deutsche Bank and Postbank branches, and the German deposit guarantee. The website and app are in German.
- **Revolut Business:** strong for multiple currencies and international payments, and held at Revolut Bank in Lithuania with the Lithuanian deposit guarantee. Business accounts for Germany may come with a Lithuanian IBAN rather than a German one, so check before you open one.
- **Wise Business:** the cheapest way for many freelancers to receive money from clients outside the eurozone, with local account details in several currencies. Wise is an e-money institution regulated in Belgium, so there is no deposit guarantee. Many freelancers use it alongside a German business account rather than instead of one.
- **Accountable:** the paid plans of this accounting app include a free business account, which makes sense if you use <AffiliateLink slug="accountable">Accountable</AffiliateLink> for your bookkeeping anyway.

## Which one fits you

- **You want a free account and nothing else:** N26 Business Standard. Simple, from a German bank, in English.
- **You want help setting money aside for taxes:** Kontist. Read more about [tax prepayments](/taxes/tax-prepayments) and why they catch new freelancers out.
- **You send many invoices to German businesses:** Qonto, with e-invoicing on every plan.
- **You want lots of features for free and keep small balances:** Finom.
- **Your clients pay in dollars or pounds:** a German business account plus Wise Business for receiving foreign currencies.
- **You are just starting out:** see the full checklist in [how to become a freelancer in Germany](/start/become-a-freelancer).

## Opening an account

Most app banks let you open an account in a few minutes with your smartphone and a valid ID. Expect to be asked for your address in Germany and your tax ID (<Term de="Steuer-ID" />), and sometimes your tax number once you have it. Most business accounts are only for people who live in Germany.

Account fees are business expenses, so they reduce your taxable profit: see [deductible expenses for freelancers](/taxes/deductible-expenses-for-freelancers). If you are a <Term de="Kleinunternehmer" />, you pay the price including VAT.
