<ShortAnswer>
If you are not a citizen of the EU, the EEA or Switzerland, you need a residence permit that allows self-employment before you start freelancing in Germany. Freelancers in liberal professions (<Term de="Freiberufler" />) apply under § 21(5) of the Residence Act, which skips the economic-interest test that trade businesses face. You must show that you can support yourself, have health insurance, hold any licence your profession needs, and, if you are older than 45, have an adequate pension. Expect a permit for one to three years at first.
</ShortAnswer>

## Who needs a permit to freelance?

- **EU, EEA and Swiss citizens:** no permit. Free movement covers self-employment, so you only register your address (<Term de="Anmeldung" />) and with the tax office.
- **Everyone else:** you need a residence permit (<Term de="Aufenthaltserlaubnis" />) that allows self-employment. Starting to freelance without one is not allowed, even if you are in Germany legally for another reason.

If you already live in Germany with a permit for another purpose, such as a job, studies or family, look at the note on your permit. Under the Residence Act, a residence permit allows work unless the law limits it, and every permit must state whether work is allowed. If it says "Erwerbstätigkeit erlaubt" (gainful activity allowed), you may freelance. If it is tied to a particular job, as it often is for employees, you need permission from the immigration office (<Term de="Ausländerbehörde" />) first. The office can allow self-employment alongside your existing purpose.

The opportunity card (<Term de="Chancenkarte" />) is not a route into freelancing: it only allows employment of up to 20 hours a week on average and short trial work.

## Freelancer or business founder?

The Residence Act has two main routes for self-employment, and which one applies to you makes a big difference:

<ComparisonTable>

| | Freelancer in a liberal profession | Trade business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal basis | § 21(5) AufenthG | § 21(1) AufenthG |
| Economic interest or regional need | Not required | Required, with positive effects on the economy expected |
| Financing | You must show you can support yourself and fund your work | Must be secured by your own capital or a loan commitment |
| Chambers and authorities | Professional bodies are consulted | Asked for an opinion on your business plan |
| Settlement permit | Under the general rules, usually after five years | Possible after three years if the business is successful |
| Best for | Developers, designers, writers, translators, consultants and other liberal professions | Shops, agencies with staff, trades and other commercial businesses |

</ComparisonTable>

Whether your work counts as freelance generally follows the list of liberal professions in tax law, the same test the tax office uses. The English word "freelancer" does not automatically mean Freiberufler in this sense. Read [Freiberufler vs. Gewerbe](/start/freiberufler-vs-gewerbe) before you apply, because applying under the wrong route is a common reason for delays.

There are also easier routes for some people. Graduates of a German university, and researchers and Blue Card holders (<Term de="Blaue Karte EU" />), should normally be given a self-employment permit if the work relates to their studies or research.

## What are the requirements?

For a freelance permit under § 21(5), the immigration office checks:

- **That you can support yourself.** Your expected income must cover your living costs, rent and insurance. Offices look at signed contracts or letters of intent from clients, your revenue forecast and your savings.
- **Health insurance.** Public or private German health insurance. Travel insurance and most foreign policies are not enough. See the [health insurance guide for freelancers](/health-insurance/freelancers), including who can join the public system.
- **A professional licence, if your profession needs one,** for example as a doctor or lawyer. It must be granted or at least promised.
- **An adequate pension if you are older than 45.** The law says that applicants older than 45 should only get the permit if they have adequate retirement provision. Each state sets its own figures. In Berlin, since 1 July 2025, you need a prospective monthly pension of €1,612.53 from age 67, for at least 12 years, or assets of €232,204.
- **The general conditions for any permit,** such as a valid passport and no grounds for expulsion.

<Callout title="Don't start before the permit">
Working before your permit allows it can cost you the permit and lead to a fine. If you are already in Germany and your current permit doesn't allow self-employment, wait for the new permit or the office's written permission before you invoice your first client.
</Callout>

## Which documents do you need?

Offices set their own lists, so check the website of the immigration office for the place you will live. Berlin's list for freelancers is typical:

- Passport, and your current residence title if you have one
- CV with proof of your qualifications and professional experience
- A revenue forecast (Berlin has its own form)
- Fee agreements with clients, or at least two letters of intent that describe the work and its scope
- A professional licence, if your profession needs one
- For artists and language teachers: proof of other regular income, such as savings or a commitment from someone who supports you
- Proof of health insurance: for public insurance a confirmation with the contribution amount, or proof of membership of the <Term de="Künstlersozialkasse" />; for private insurance a certificate of cover and costs
- Your rental contract or proof of home ownership, with proof of the monthly costs
- Proof of pension provision, if you are older than 45

In Berlin, applications for a freelance permit are online only.

## How do you apply?

**From abroad.** Most people apply for a national visa (category D) for freelance work at the German embassy or consulate in their country. The embassy asks the immigration office for the place you plan to live to agree, so the requirements are checked before you travel. In Germany, you then register your address and collect the residence permit.

**From inside Germany.** Citizens of Australia, Canada, Israel, Japan, New Zealand, South Korea, the United Kingdom and the United States can enter without a visa and apply for the residence permit at the local immigration office. They must apply within 90 days of arriving, and may not work until the permit allows it. People who already live in Germany with another permit apply to their local immigration office to change or add a purpose.

Once you have the permit, you register with the tax office. That step is the same for everyone: see [the tax registration questionnaire in English](/start/tax-registration-questionnaire) and the full checklist in [how to become a freelancer in Germany](/start/become-a-freelancer).

## What does it cost?

The fees are set nationally:

- **National visa (category D):** €75
- **Residence permit:** €100 when it is first issued
- **Extension:** €93 for an extension of more than three months
- **Change of purpose,** for example from a job permit to freelancing: €98

Some offices charge less when the permit is issued as a sticker in your passport rather than as a card. Translations, certified copies, a tax advisor's statement or a lawyer cost extra.

## How long is it valid, and what comes next?

Offices usually issue the first freelance permit for one to three years. To extend it, you show that your freelance income covers your living costs. Berlin, for example, asks freelancers for their latest tax assessment, a profit statement from a tax advisor and six months of bank statements showing income from the work.

The three-year shortcut to permanent residence in § 21(4) applies only to business founders, not to freelancers. As a freelancer you can apply for a settlement permit (<Term de="Niederlassungserlaubnis" />) under the general rules, which include:

- five years with a residence permit,
- a secure income,
- 60 months of contributions to the public pension, or proof of comparable private provision,
- German at level B1, and basic knowledge of the legal and social system, for example through the integration course,
- enough living space for you and your family.

Most freelancers don't have to pay into the public pension, so plan early how you will show "comparable provision", for example with a private pension or voluntary public contributions.

## Should you get help?

Many freelancers apply on their own, especially with a clear profession and signed client contracts. Professional help is worth it if your work sits between freelance and trade, if you are over 45 and your pension provision is borderline, or if an application has been refused before. A lawyer specialising in migration law can represent you; tax advisors can prepare the revenue forecast and the profit statements offices ask for.
