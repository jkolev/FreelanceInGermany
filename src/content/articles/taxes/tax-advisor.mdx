<ShortAnswer>
No law requires a freelancer in Germany to use a tax advisor (<Term de="Steuerberater" />). Many people with simple finances file their own returns with software. An advisor is worth it in your first year, if you run a trade, employ staff, work across borders or face a big decision. Fees follow official rules (the <Term de="StBVV" />); for a small freelance business, annual returns typically cost several hundred to around €1,500 a year, and more with ongoing bookkeeping. The business part of the fee is deductible. To find an English-speaking advisor, use a directory with a language filter, then check the official register.
</ShortAnswer>

## Do you have to use a tax advisor?

No. As a freelancer you may do your own taxes: the registration questionnaire, your VAT returns, the profit calculation (<Term de="Einnahmenüberschussrechnung" />, EÜR) and your income tax return, all through <Term de="ELSTER" />. Plenty of freelancers do, especially in the first years. [Taxes for freelancers in Germany](/taxes/freelancer-taxes) gives an overview of what that involves.

Doing it yourself works best when your situation is simple: one kind of work, clients mainly in Germany, few large purchases, and bookkeeping you keep up with during the year. If you use the [small business VAT exemption](/taxes/kleinunternehmer), there are no VAT returns either, which removes a large part of the work.

## When is a tax advisor worth it?

An advisor tends to pay off in these situations:

- **Your first year.** The registration questionnaire asks for profit estimates and VAT choices that affect your [prepayments](/taxes/tax-prepayments) and are hard to undo. One early consultation can prevent expensive mistakes.
- **You're not sure if you're a Freiberufler or a Gewerbe.** The answer decides whether you pay trade tax, and the tax office can change it later. [Freiberufler vs. Gewerbe](/start/freiberufler-vs-gewerbe) explains the test.
- **You run a trade business** (<Term de="Gewerbe" />), with a trade tax return on top of everything else.
- **You employ people.** Payroll has its own rules and deadlines.
- **You work across borders**: clients in several countries, reverse-charge invoices, or a move into or out of Germany during the year.
- **Big purchases or a company car**, where depreciation and private use need careful handling.
- **The tax office challenges you**, for example with an audit, an estimate or a demand you think is wrong.
- **You set up a company** such as a GmbH, which needs a balance sheet.

## What are your options?

Income tax aid associations (<Term de="Lohnsteuerhilfeverein" />), the low-cost tax help for employees, are not an option. They may not help anyone with income from self-employment, not even a small side income, and not even with the employee part of the return (§ 4 Nr. 11 StBerG).

That leaves three realistic setups:

<ComparisonTable>

| | Do it yourself with software | Advisor for annual returns only | Full-service advisor |
|---|---|---|---|
| What you do | Everything: bookkeeping, VAT returns, annual returns | Bookkeeping and VAT returns during the year | Hand over receipts and bank data |
| What the advisor does | Nothing | EÜR and income tax return once a year | Bookkeeping, VAT returns and annual returns |
| Typical cost per year | Software subscription | Several hundred to around €1,500 | Often €1,500 to €5,000 |
| Deadline for annual returns | 31 July of the following year | End of February of the second following year | End of February of the second following year |
| Main downside | Mistakes are yours to find | You still need tidy records all year | The highest cost |
| Best for | Simple finances and time to learn | Most freelancers after the first year | Trades, employees, high turnover or little time |

</ComparisonTable>

If you do it yourself, an accounting app such as <AffiliateLink slug="accountable">Accountable</AffiliateLink> can track income and expenses and help with [VAT returns](/taxes/vat-returns) and the EÜR. Many freelancers also combine options: they keep their own books in an app and pay an advisor only for the annual returns.

## What does a tax advisor cost?

German tax advisors are paid under an official fee schedule, the Steuerberatervergütungsverordnung (StBVV). It was last raised on 1 July 2025. It allows three ways of charging:

- **Value-based fees.** Most tasks are billed as a fraction of a table fee that grows with the amounts involved. For the EÜR, the basis is your business income or expenses, whichever is higher. For the income tax return, it is your income. The advisor chooses the fraction within a set range, depending on how much work your case is.
- **Time-based fees.** For advice that doesn't fit the tables: €16.50 to €41 per started quarter hour, which is €66 to €164 an hour, plus VAT.
- **Agreed fees.** Advisors may agree a flat monthly or annual fee with you, which is common for ongoing bookkeeping. They may also agree in writing to charge more than the tables.

On top come a flat fee for expenses (20 percent, at most €20 per invoice) and 19 percent VAT.

What that means in practice, as a rough guide:

- **Small freelance business, annual returns only** (EÜR plus income tax return): several hundred euros to around €1,500 a year.
- **With ongoing bookkeeping and VAT returns:** often €1,500 to €5,000 a year.

Your price depends mostly on how much work your records cause. A shoebox of paper receipts costs more than a tidy export from an accounting app. Ask for a written quote before you start.

## Can you deduct the fees?

Partly. The fee for business tasks is a deductible business expense: bookkeeping, the EÜR, VAT returns, the trade tax return and advice on them. The fee for the private parts of your income tax return is not, for example the main form, health insurance and pension contributions, or childcare costs.

If an invoice mixes both, split it with a reasonable estimate. For mixed fees of up to €100 a year, the tax office accepts your split. See [deductible expenses for freelancers](/taxes/deductible-expenses-for-freelancers) for everything else you can deduct.

## How do you find an English-speaking tax advisor?

"Steuerberater" is a protected title in Germany, and only licensed advisors may give full tax advice. Many websites offer "tax consulting" in English without that license, so always check.

1. **Search a directory with a language filter.** The chambers' own search service, which had a language filter, closed at the end of 2025. Private directories such as [Steuerberater-Wegweiser](https://steuerberater-wegweiser.de/steuerberatung/sprachen-englisch) let you filter for English and your city. Expat communities and city guides also keep lists, and colleagues in your field are often the best source.
2. **Check the license.** Look the person or firm up in the [official register of tax advisors](https://www.verzeichnis-der-steuerberater.de/) run by the Federal Chamber of Tax Advisors. It's free, and it shows whether someone is licensed. It has no language filter, so use it to check, not to search.
3. **Ask the right questions before you sign:**
   - Do you work with freelancers in my field, and with clients like me (for example foreign clients or the small business exemption)?
   - Do you write in English too, or only speak it?
   - How do you charge, and what will my first year cost?
   - How do I send documents? Most advisors use a digital system, often from DATEV.
   - Who is my contact, and how fast do you usually reply?

## What stays your responsibility?

Even with an advisor, the tax office deals with you. You provide complete documents on time, you check what the advisor prepares, and you remain responsible for the figures. A good advisor explains what they filed and why, in a language you understand.
